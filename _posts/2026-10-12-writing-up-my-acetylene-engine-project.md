In 2018–19, my Bachelor’s project team wrote a report comparing petrol and acetylene fuel use in a small spark-ignition engine. When I revisited the work for this portfolio, I wanted to separate the readings from the report’s broader claims.

## Framing the study

The report describes a single-cylinder, four-stroke, 106 cc Yamaha Libero engine. We compared the reported fuel-consumption values at four engine speeds: 700, 1,000, 1,300, and 1,800 rpm. The report also discusses brake-specific fuel consumption and exhaust-gas temperature.

## Fuel-consumption readings

| Engine speed | Petrol (g/s) | Acetylene (g/s) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 700 rpm | 0.0676 | 0.0615 |
| 1,000 rpm | 0.0810 | 0.0730 |
| 1,300 rpm | 0.0950 | 0.0864 |
| 1,800 rpm | 0.1095 | 0.0996 |

</div>

The point-by-point reductions were 9.0%, 9.9%, 9.1%, and 9.0%, for a mean of 9.25%. The report rounds the result to about 9% lower. That is a summary of this experiment, not a claim about road mileage, present-day fuel costs, or performance in other engines.

## What I would make clearer in the report

The test covered one engine and four speeds. The report does not show repeat-run variability, so the figures should be read as a limited comparison rather than a general performance claim. Its cost-per-kilometre section also relies on simplified operating and price assumptions; it is not a current economic assessment.

The report records detonation above 1,800 rpm. I treat that as a limitation noted in this historical study, not as a safe operating limit or an instruction for running an engine.

If I were writing the report again, I would keep the question narrow, show the measured values beside each test point, and separate measured results from estimates. I would also state the test limits beside the conclusion so readers do not mistake a small comparison for proof of practical fuel viability.

This post is a retrospective on an academic report, not an operating guide.
